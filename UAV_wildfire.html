Research

Wildfire Remote Sensing

Drones, AI, and GIS for prescribed fire mapping, home wildfire risk, and forest recovery after fire.

Funded by NASA through the FireSage program

GeoFly Lab uses drone technology to better understand wildfire behavior and to support prescribed fire research. Working with CAL FIRE and the Wildfire Interdisciplinary Research Center (WIRC), we fly thermal, multispectral, and LiDAR drones before, during, and after fire events to capture fire dynamics that are often invisible to the eye.

Project 1

Prescribed fire mapping

Seeing through smoke with thermal drones and AI

During prescribed burns, our thermal drones see through dense smoke and record the structure of active fire in real time. The georeferenced thermal video reveals flame intensity and spread at fine spatial scales. Combined with fuel type, elevation, and wind measurements, these data feed GIS-based models that simulate fire progression and improve prediction.

AI detection of the fire front

Firefighters on the ground often cannot see through smoke to locate the fire front. We train computer vision models on thermal drone video to detect the fire head automatically, giving crews precise, real-time information on where the fire is moving. The same thermal data serve as training sets for automated analysis of fire behavior.

Fuels and burn severity

LiDAR sensors map fuel volume and measure fuel change before and after burns, quantifying vegetation structure and fire impact across the landscape. Multispectral sensors, including near-infrared bands, track vegetation health, burn severity, and recovery over time.

Together with weather tower data and high-resolution RGB imagery, these sensors form a multi-sensor platform that supports prescribed fire management and broader wildfire resilience.

Data collection was led by Owen Hussey (M.A. Geography, 2025), and data analysis by Dr. Xiangyu Ren, who applied AI and GIS methods to turn raw imagery into fire science insights.

Project 2

Home Ignition Zone (HIZ)

Scaling up home wildfire-risk assessment with drones

The Home Ignition Zone project is a collaboration between GeoFly Lab and the Wilkin Fire Ecology Lab. Building on earlier on-the-ground surveys of conditions around homes, we now use drones and remote sensing to assess wildfire risk across whole communities, with faster, more consistent, and more scalable methods than traditional field evaluations.

The research is closely integrated with WIRC, CAL FIRE, and industry mentors through the NSF Industry–University Cooperative Research Center (IUCRC) program, linking the science to practical applications in insurance, utility management, and state wildfire planning.

Study sites include fire-affected and high-risk communities such as Paradise, Tahoe Donner, and Santa Cruz. We collect high-resolution drone imagery and thermal data to map vegetation, building materials, and potential ember pathways, and combine them with NASA satellite time series and street-level imagery for a multi-scale view of risk. Pairing drone products with curbside and full-property evaluations captures details that standard defensible-space inspections often miss, such as vent mesh size or gaps in construction materials.

Communities and students

Community engagement is central to the project. We talk with residents about wildfire risk, home hardening, and the barriers they face in carrying out mitigation, so our recommendations are practical and specific to each community.

The project is closely tied to the NASA FireSage program, in which student interns analyze the drone and satellite data collected at HIZ sites and gain hands-on experience with thermal, multispectral, and LiDAR analysis.

HIZ fieldwork was led by Henri Brillon (M.A. Geography, 2025), who coordinated site evaluations and connected community surveys with geospatial data collection.

Project 3

Post-burn recovery at San Vicente Redwoods

Remote sensing and field science to guide forest recovery after wildfire

San Vicente Redwoods is one of the most ecologically important coastal mixed evergreen forests in Northern California. After the 2020 CZU Lightning Complex fire, GeoFly Lab and partners at WIRC, NASA Ames, and San José State University have been studying how fire severity affects forest structure, aboveground biomass, and long-term recovery.

We combine remote sensing, LiDAR, and Continuous Forest Inventory plot data. Using NASA GEDI LiDAR and Sentinel-2 imagery, we map burn severity and track aboveground biomass before the fire, one year after, and three years after, revealing clear links between fire severity and tree mortality.

Drone multispectral and LiDAR surveys complement the satellite data by capturing fine-scale change in vegetation and canopy structure. This lets us quantify biomass loss accurately and follow species-specific recovery: coast live oak and Douglas-fir showed high mortality under high burn severity, while tanoak and madrone resprouted differently in moderate burn areas.

Post-burn work at San Vicente Redwoods is about more than documenting damage. By linking field ecology, remote sensing, and fire science, it gives land managers, conservation groups, and policymakers the data they need for forest restoration and carbon management in the Santa Cruz Mountains.

The project is also a training ground for students through the NASA FireSage program, where interns and graduate researchers analyze drone and satellite data to study wildfire impacts.

This work was led by student researcher Melina Kompella.
